# Writing

Text that reads like an AI wrote it costs more attention than blunt, specific text, however
polished it feels while writing it. Write as if a person who knows the subject were typing fast,
not composing a keynote.

Consult this before the first sentence exists, not after the draft does. Every tell below is
cheaper to not write than to find and remove later.

## 1. Two hard failures

An em dash (`—`) anywhere in the text, and an emoji anywhere in the text, including as a bullet
marker or a sign-off. Some projects enforce these with a prose check that fails on either. Treat
them as enforced whether or not this project has one. Neither is ever worth an exception: split
the sentence instead of the em dash, and cut the emoji instead of trading it for a different one.

If there is a prose check, run it last. It catches these two and nothing that follows. Everything
past this point is what to read for yourself.

## 2. Punctuation and formatting tells

- An em dash as a crutch to join two clauses. Catch it while writing by reading the sentence back;
  a period, a colon, or a rewrite always works.
- Bold or italic used to fake emphasis on a phrase that would carry the emphasis on its own if it
  were the sentence's last words. Plenty of places render the markers literally anyway.
- Semicolons or a dash stacking two sentences that would read better as two sentences.
- Headers, bullets and bold on text a person would have written as two paragraphs.
- A dramatic pause built out of punctuation: "Here's the thing.", "The result?", "And the best
  part?"

## 3. Banned or overused words and constructions

| Avoid                                                                       | Say instead, or cut it                             |
| --------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- |
| revolutionary, innovative, robust, comprehensive, seamless, game-changing    | the plain, specific claim: what it actually does   |
| "in today's world", "in the digital age", "in a fast-paced landscape"       | drop the opener, start on the actual sentence      |
| "it's important to note that", "it's worth mentioning", "notably"           | just state it                                      |
| "it's not just X, it's Y", "not only X but also Y"                          | pick the one true claim                            |
| "whether you're A, B, or C"                                                 | name the one reader this is actually for           |
| "dive into", "explore the world of", "unlock", "unleash", "elevate"         | "open", "look at", "start", or the concrete action |
| "in summary", "ultimately", "at the end of the day", "all in all"           | end the paragraph on the last real point instead   |
| "a true journey", "a testament to"                                          | the concrete fact that would make the claim true   |
| "delve", "leverage", "utilize", "streamline", "empower"                     | dig, use, use, simplify, let                       |

This list is a start, not a wall. The tell is the shape (a vague elevation, a hedge, a stock
transition, a false contrast), not the exact string. A phrase not on this table can still be the
same move, and the move survives translation: in any other language the local equivalents of these
are cut the same way.

## 4. Structural and rhythm tells

- Everything arriving in exactly three items: three examples, three clauses, three adjectives in a
  row. Real writing lands on two, four, seven; vary it on purpose.
- Every sentence the same length and the same shape (subject, verb, object, subject, verb, object).
  Break the pattern the way a person naturally would: a short sentence after two long ones, or a
  fragment where it lands harder than a full clause.
- A tidy summary sentence closing every paragraph. Let some paragraphs just end on the fact.
- A rhetorical question the next sentence answers.
- Opening by restating the topic or the request before saying anything about it.
- Hedging every claim ("can help", "tends to", "generally") when the text is about something the
  writer knows firsthand. Say the specific thing plainly.

## 5. Content tells

- A vague superlative instead of one checkable fact: "much faster" says nothing next to "from 4
  seconds to under 1". Prefer the number, the date, the name.
- Blandness that would survive swapping the subject for any other. If the sentence still holds
  after a find-and-replace on the name, it says nothing about this one.
- Enthusiasm that outruns the claim ("incredible", "mind-blowing") standing in for the actual
  reason something is good.
- Telling the reader what they will feel ("you'll love this", "you won't believe") instead of
  giving them the thing and letting them decide.
- Explaining what the reader already knows about their own field before getting to the point.

## 6. Before calling it finished

Read it back once, out loud if possible, and ask: would a person actually say this sentence, in
this order, to someone they know? If a sentence is there only to fill the shape, cut it. Then run
the prose check if there is one. It catches the em dash and the emoji, not the rest.
